n Systems, m Attackers: Penetration Scores, LLN and CLT

The penetration process as a random walk with i.i.d. increments: simulated trajectories, the three normalizations Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n, the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem.

1. Model 2. What to expect 3. Trajectories 4. Increasing n 5. CLT 6. The role of m 7. Interpretation

1. The model

There are n systems and m attackers. Each attacker works through the n systems one after the other; at system i it obtains a penetration score Xi. The scores are independent and identically distributed, both across systems and across attackers, so every attacker produces one independent trajectory of the partial sums

Sk = X1 + X2 + … + Xk,   k = 1, …, n

and the m attackers give m independent samples of every quantity we study.

Bernoulli model

Xi ∈ {0, 1}, P(Xi = 1) = p.
1 = system i was penetrated, 0 = the attack failed. Sn counts the penetrated systems and has a Binomial(n, p) distribution.
μ = E[Xi] = p,   σ² = Var(Xi) = p(1 − p).

Rademacher model

Xi ∈ {−1, +1}, each with probability ½.
+1 = the attacker gains ground, −1 = it is detected and loses ground. Sn is a symmetric random walk, (Sn + n)/2 ~ Binomial(n, ½).
μ = 0,   σ² = 1: the symmetric, zero-mean case.

2. What the theory predicts

By linearity of expectation and independence of the increments, E[Sn] = nμ and Var(Sn) = nσ². Dividing by a constant c divides the mean by c and the variance by c²:

Weak Law of Large Numbers

For every ε > 0:   P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0.
Proof via Chebyshev: P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ Var(Sn/n)/ε² = σ²/(nε²).

Central Limit Theorem

(Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution.
Rademacher: μ = 0, σ = 1, so Sn/√n itself → N(0, 1). Bernoulli: Sn/√n must first be centred and scaled: (Sn − np)/√(np(1−p)).

The three normalizations correspond to three scales: dividing by 1 keeps the fluctuations (√n) and the drift (n); dividing by n kills the fluctuations and keeps the drift (LLN); dividing by √n keeps the fluctuations at a constant size, which is exactly the scale at which their shape becomes Gaussian (CLT).

4. Increasing n

One simulation with m attackers over nmax systems; Sn is recorded at n = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, … for every attacker.

5. Convergence in distribution: the Central Limit Theorem

  • For small n the distribution of Sn/√n is visibly discrete (S1/√1 takes only the values ±1) and the histogram looks nothing like a bell. Already for n ≈ 30 it is close to N(0, 1), and the KS distance falls as 1/√n.
  • For the Bernoulli model with small p the convergence is slower: the distribution is skewed (skewness (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p))), because penetrations are rare events and the Poisson regime dominates until np is large.
  • Convergence in distribution is a statement about the shape of the distribution, not about single trajectories: a given attacker's Sn/√n does not settle anywhere, it keeps fluctuating (see the trajectories in section 3).

6. The role of the number of attackers m

n and m play different roles. n is the length of each trajectory, the asymptotic variable of the LLN and CLT. m is the number of independent copies: it does not change the distribution of Sn, but it decides how well we can estimate it and how extreme the best attacker is.

7. Interpretation: system size and number of attackers

  • Total score Sn: larger systems are penetrated more, and less predictably in absolute terms. In the Bernoulli model an attacker penetrates on average np of the n systems; the uncertainty around that number grows as √(np(1 − p)). Doubling the infrastructure doubles the expected damage but increases its standard deviation only by √2.
  • Average score Sn/n: the penetration rate becomes predictable (LLN). The fraction of systems an attacker breaks converges to p: for a large infrastructure the per-system rate measured on one attacker is a reliable estimate of the true vulnerability p, with error of order √(p(1 − p)/n). This is the justification for measuring security with averages over many systems.
  • Normalized score Sn/√n: the fluctuations have a universal shape (CLT). Whatever the single-step distribution (0/1 or ±1), deviations from the mean are Gaussian on the √n scale. In the symmetric Rademacher case there is no drift: an attacker who is as often repelled as successful is, on average, nowhere, yet its score wanders away from 0 by about √n, and its sign at the end is a coin toss.
  • More attackers do not change the distribution, they sample its tail. Each attacker's score has the same law; what grows with m is the best score, roughly nμ + σ√n·√(2 ln m) (a slowly growing, logarithmic effect). Against a large population of attackers the defender should plan for the maximum, not the mean: the best attacker among 10 000 sits about 3.9 standard deviations above the average one. The total over all attackers, Σj Sn(j), is itself a sum of nm i.i.d. increments: mean nmμ, standard deviation σ√(nm).
  • More attackers give better estimates. The error of any estimate from the m trajectories (mean, variance, probabilities, the shape of the histogram) decreases as 1/√m. A “suitable” m is therefore chosen for the precision required: m ≈ 2000–5000 resolves probabilities of about 1% and KS distances of about 0.02; to see the CLT at large n we need m large enough that the Monte Carlo noise 0.87/√m is below the true distance from the normal.
